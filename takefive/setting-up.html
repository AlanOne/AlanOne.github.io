Getting started

Open Take 5 for the first time

  1. On a Mac (macOS 11 or later, Intel or Apple silicon), open the disk image: it holds Take 5, a shortcut to Applications, Read Me First.txt and the Take 5 Demo song. Drag Take 5 onto Applications. The first time, macOS asks you to confirm an app that isn't from the App Store: on macOS 11 to 14, CtrlControl-click Take 5 and choose Open; on macOS 15 or later, double-click it, click Done, then go to System Settings > Privacy & Security and click Open Anyway. If macOS says the app "is damaged", Read Me First has the one line to paste into Terminal.
  2. On Windows (10 or 11, 64-bit), run the installer. It asks whether to install just for you (no administrator password) or for everyone on the computer (needs one), offers a desktop icon, adds Take 5 to the Start menu, makes song packages (.take5song) and parts (.take5parts) open in Take 5, and can start it when it's done. If Windows says "Windows protected your PC", click More info, then Run anyway. A newer installer installs over the old version, copying only what changed; if Take 5 is open it asks to close it first (Take 5 asks about unsaved songs).
  3. When Take 5 asks to use the microphone (Mac), click Allow: it can't record without it. If you clicked Don't Allow, turn Take 5 on in System Settings > Privacy & Security > Microphone.
  4. Take 5 opens the song you had open last time. The very first time there's none, so it makes a new one, "Untitled Song", in Music/Take 5 (a real song folder: keep it or delete it), and a short guided tour starts a second later (see "Take the guided tour"). If Take 5 closed without saving last time, it offers the changes it kept (see "Recover after a crash").
  5. Choose your audio interface or headphones in Settings > Audio Settings..., Ctrl,Cmd,. See "Set up an audio interface".

Update Take 5 (Check for Updates)

Each time Take 5 opens, it first asks the internet whether there's a newer Take 5 (in the background). If there is, a window says An update is available, with what's new in it (its release notes: what's new, changed and fixed; scroll for more) and two buttons: Install Now and Close (which leaves it for now). Help > Check for Updates... (on the Mac, Take 5 > Check for Updates...) asks any time: with nothing newer the status line says which version is the latest, and without internet the window says it couldn't check. Install Now downloads it with a progress bar (Cancel stops it) and checks it's exactly the file that was published (its size and fingerprint), so a broken or altered download is never run. Then:

  • On Windows, Take 5 closes (asking about unsaved changes first), the installer runs by itself and Take 5 opens again, in a minute or so. Your songs and settings stay as they were.
  • On the Mac, the disk image opens in Finder: quit Take 5, drag the new Take 5 onto Applications (replacing the old one) and open it.

The downloaded installer (a few hundred MB) is deleted the next time Take 5 opens, once that version (or a newer one) is the one running. The check when Take 5 opens says nothing unless there's a newer version; Check for Updates at Startup, next to it, turns it off. Which version you have is in Help > About Take 5 (Take 5 > About Take 5), with the build it is ("Take 5 v0.17.1"). The online cheat sheet and manual (alanone.github.io/takefive) links to the latest under its title (Windows installer, Mac disk image, All releases).

Take the guided tour (tutorial)

Help > Tutorial starts a tour of the basics on the song that's open, in 13 steps. It highlights the real controls one at a time: playing and recording, adding a track, a track's controls, plugins, instrument tracks, writing notes, the mixer, recording takes, saving and exporting, and making Take 5 your own. Do what a step says (it moves on by itself once it's done), or press Next; on the last step it's Finish. Back goes to the previous step and Skip tour ends it. The tour runs on its own the first time Take 5 starts, and Help > Tutorial opens it again any time a song is open. The keys it mentions follow your chosen Key Commands set. It's safe on a song you care about: the steps that change something (adding a track, writing notes) can be undone with CtrlZCmdZ.

Find where my songs are saved

Each song is a folder of its own in your Music folder, under Take 5 (Music/Take 5, Music\Take 5 on Windows). Inside it:

  • the song file, ending in .tracktionedit (open this one);
  • Audio: every recording and imported file;
  • Backups: earlier saves, one at most every 5 minutes, the newest 20 kept (File > Revert to Backup lists them by date);
  • Snapshots, if you've saved any, and Captures, the NAM captures and impulse responses copied into the song;
  • a hidden autosave, written every 2 minutes while there are unsaved changes (not while recording), which brings the work back after a crash.

Recordings always go into the song's Audio folder, so the folder can be copied or moved as a whole, or zipped and sent (or use File > Save Song Package...). File > Show Song Folder opens it in Finder (Explorer on Windows). What isn't in the song lives in Take 5's settings (~/Library/Take 5, %APPDATA%\Take 5 on Windows): song and track templates, layouts, macros, your key commands and the audio settings.

Start a new song

File > New Song..., CtrlNCmdN, asks where to make the song: it starts in Music/Take 5 with the name "New Song". Type a name and save; Take 5 makes a folder by that name (it won't use a folder that already has files in it). A new song starts at 120 BPM in 4/4, with the click on, a one-bar count-in and one audio track. To start from your own setup instead (the band's tracks, inputs, plugins, buses and routing), make a song the way you like it and choose File > Save as Song Template...: it keeps everything but the clips. Then:

  • Settings > Template for New Songs picks the one File > New Song starts from; None (one audio track) goes back to the plain song.
  • File > New Song from Template picks any of them for this one song (greyed when you haven't saved one).

Open a song

File > Open Song..., CtrlOCmdO, starts in Music/Take 5: open a song folder's .tracktionedit file, or a song package (.take5song) a bandmate sent you (it's unpacked into Music/Take 5 first). File > Open Recent lists the songs you've opened, by name (with the folder too when two have the same name; songs that no longer exist are left out; Clear Menu empties it). Take 5 reopens the last song by itself when it starts. If the song open now has unsaved changes, Take 5 asks whether to save them first. Double-clicking a song or a package in Finder (Explorer on Windows) while Take 5 is open opens it in that window, not in a second Take 5. If files the song uses have moved, Take 5 says how many are missing: Find in Folder... looks for them in a folder you choose, Later opens the song without them (see "Find files a song is missing").

Open the demo song

The demo song "Take 5 Demo" plays on Take 5's own sounds (the Amp, the Drum Machine, the built-in synth), so it needs nothing else installed. It's a folder like any song's: the song, its Audio and the drum kit's Samples.

  • On the Mac, drag the "Take 5 Demo" folder from the disk image into Music/Take 5 (where Open Song starts).
  • On Windows the installer puts it in Music\Take 5\Take 5 Demo. Installing again doesn't overwrite a demo you've changed, and uninstalling leaves it.

Then File > Open Song... and open "Take 5 Demo.tracktionedit" inside it. Play with it freely: to keep the demo as it came, copy its folder first and change the copy, or don't save.

Know the parts of the window

  • The top bars: the transport, with the click volume and Count-in at the left; go to start, play and record; loop, punch and the metronome; the display with bar, beat and tick, time, tempo and time signature; the output loudness meter at the right. In the Console layout a toolbar, a status line and an info line come under it (see "Work in the Console layout"), and any layout can have them (see "Arrange the top bars"). In a window too narrow for a row, what doesn't fit goes onto a line under it.
  • Track headers (left): name, input, R (arm), M (mute), S (solo), the headphones (monitoring), A (automation), the fader, pan, meter and plugin slots.
  • The ruler and the lanes above the tracks: tempo, sections, chords, lyrics and the reference track. Each lane's header (in the track headers' column) shows what it says at the playhead: the time signature and tempo, the section, the chord. Hover a header and click its x to hide the lane; View > Lanes > Tempo Lane (G), Sections Lane (ShiftG), Chord Lane (AltGOptionG) and Reference Lane (AltShiftGShiftOptionG) show and hide them.
  • The tracks with their clips; below or instead of them, the mixer and the MIDI editor or Pitch editor.
  • Side panels on the right: the shortcuts, the audio file browser, notes, the assistant, the test checklist, smart controls, modulators and CPU use (View > Panels).
  • The Inspector on the left (I): the selected track's and clip's settings (see "See a track's settings in the Inspector").
  • The status line at the bottom: at the left, what just happened and hints; at the right, the audio driver and interface, the sample rate, the buffer size (and the delay it makes), the inputs and outputs, and how much of the computer's time the song's audio takes (CPU). Click the right half to see which tracks and plugins use the most (see "Find what's eating the CPU").

Find any command or shortcut

The menus keep the commands used most at the top level and the rest in submenus by kind: in View, Lanes, Panels, Windows, Top Bars, Zoom and Automation; Edit > Tools and Edit > Edit Options; File > Export Other Formats; Track > Other New Tracks and Track > Group; Mix > Reference Track. Every command keeps its key wherever it is.

  • View > Command Palette..., CtrlShiftPShiftCmdP: type part of a command's name, the first letters of its words ("nit" finds New Instrument Track) or words from what it does; Up/Down and Return run one, Esc closes it. Each row shows the command's key, so it's also a way to learn them. Commands you've run come first (until Take 5 quits); ones that can't run right now are dimmed. A slip in the typing still finds it ("mxier", "metronme"), and so do its letters in order ("mxr"). When no command matches, it suggests the nearest ("Did you mean...") and up to four of the guide's tasks about what you typed ("tone3000" finds the NAM captures task), which open in the online manual. It finds commands, not the choices in a list: for a theme, a layout, a display size or a recent song, use its menu.
  • View > Panels > Shortcuts, Ctrl/Cmd/, also in the Help menu: the cheat sheet beside the tracks, with every key and every mouse gesture, grouped by area. It follows your Key Commands set.
  • Hover over any button for a tooltip.

Bring a song over from another DAW

File > Import Project from Another DAW... opens an .rpp project, an .als set, a DAWproject file (.dawproject, the open format many music apps write), an AAF session (.aaf, which most music apps and video editors can export) or a folder of stems and MIDI files, as a new song, made in Music/Take 5 under the original's name. Tracks, folders, names, volume, pan, mute, colours, audio clips (copied into the song) and MIDI come over, plus the starting tempo and time signature. Plugins, sends and tempo changes don't, nor automation (except from an AAF; see "Import an AAF session"). When it's done, a report says what came in and what didn't. Things to know:

  • Other apps' own project files (.logicx, .ptx, .cpr, .flp, .song, .bwproject and the like) can't be read: Take 5 says what to export instead when you choose one (an AAF, a DAWproject file, or stems and MIDI). OMF isn't supported: export AAF instead.
  • From an .als set, warped clips come in at their original speed and looped clips play once; return tracks aren't brought over.
  • From an .rpp project, items that were muted come in playing: mute them again.
  • A folder of stems has no tempo in it: set the song's tempo before editing to the grid.

While it imports, a window says which file it's copying into the song (a session's audio can be gigabytes, so it can take a while), and stays until the new song is open.

Import an AAF session

In the other app, export the session or timeline as AAF (choosing AAF where it offers AAF or OMF, with the audio embedded or next to the .aaf). In Take 5, File > Import Project from Another DAW... and pick the .aaf. Clips land where they were, with their fades, crossfades, clip gain and clip volume envelopes, track volume and pan (automation too, in the track's lanes), mute, solo and markers. If the session started at a timecode (01:00:00:00, say), the song keeps it: an AAF exported from the song starts there too, so it lines up again in the editor. If the audio isn't next to the AAF, Take 5 asks for the folder it's in; for MXF audio, choose the "MediaFiles" folder on the drive it was recorded to (the MXF audio is converted to WAV files in the song's Audio folder). The import report lists anything missing.

Audio and MIDI setup

Set up an audio interface

  1. Connect the interface (and install its driver, if it has one) before opening Take 5.
  2. Open Settings > Audio Settings..., Ctrl,Cmd,.
  3. Choose the driver and the interface: - On the Mac there's one driver (Core Audio): choose the interface as the Output and the Input. They can be different devices (the interface's inputs, the Mac's speakers), but one device for both keeps them in time. - On Windows, choose the Audio device type first: ASIO for most interfaces (see "Use ASIO on Windows"), else one of the Windows Audio types. With ASIO there's one Device list for both.
  4. Tick the input channels you'll record from (Active input channels) and the output channels you'll listen on (Active output channels). Only ticked channels show up in Take 5: an unticked input isn't in the tracks' input menus.
  5. Pick the Sample rate and the Audio buffer size (see "Choose a sample rate" and "Choose a buffer size").
  6. Test plays a short tone (440 Hz, a second, at half level) on the active outputs: turn your speakers or headphones down first. The meter next to the input shows its level: make a sound and check it moves.

The interface's name shows at the right end of the status line. Each ticked input appears in the tracks' input menus; inputs are mono, one per track, unless you make a pair one stereo input (see "Record a stereo source on one track"). Settings are remembered between launches. If the interface is unplugged while Take 5 is open, the sound moves to the computer's own output (in the Console layout the status line turns amber); plug it back in and choose it again here. There's nothing to set for MIDI: Take 5 listens to every MIDI keyboard it finds (see "Connect a MIDI keyboard"). On the Mac, Bluetooth MIDI... at the bottom pairs a Bluetooth one.

Choose a sample rate

The sample rate (how many times a second the sound is measured) is set in Settings > Audio Settings..., from the rates the interface offers. 44.1 kHz or 48 kHz is right for most music: 48 kHz is the usual one for video and most interfaces, 44.1 kHz the CD's. Higher rates (88.2, 96 kHz) take more CPU and disk for little you can hear. Choose it before recording a song, so every take is at the same rate: a recording made at another rate still plays at the right pitch, but is converted as it plays. Exports offer 44.1, 48, 88.2 or 96 kHz, starting at the interface's rate.

Choose a buffer size (latency vs dropouts)

The buffer size in Settings > Audio Settings... decides how long the sound takes to get through the computer: the list shows each size with the delay it makes ("128 samples (2.7 ms)"). Smaller is quicker (better for playing through an amp sim or monitoring a vocal) but works the computer harder; too small gives crackles and dropouts. 128 samples is a good start for recording, 64 or 32 on a fast computer with a good interface; raise it to 512 or 1024 for mixing a big song with lots of plugins. If you hear crackles, raise it one step. On the Mac the choices are powers of two (32, 64, 128...), 512 at first; with ASIO they come from the interface's driver, often set in its own Control Panel. While recording, Transport > Low-Latency Mode helps too (see "Play through plugins with less delay").

Use ASIO on Windows

For the lowest latency on Windows, choose ASIO as the Audio device type in Settings > Audio Settings... and your interface's ASIO driver as the Device (for example "Focusrite USB ASIO"). The interface's own control panel (buffer size and so on) opens from the Control Panel button there, and Reset Device restarts it if it stops making sound. ASIO is built into Take 5: nothing extra to install but the interface's own driver. Without one, the Windows types work with any sound card: Windows Audio (shared with other apps, about 10 ms), Windows Audio (Low Latency Mode), Windows Audio (Exclusive Mode) (lower, but no other app can play while Take 5 has it) and DirectSound (the oldest, the slowest). On a Focusrite Scarlett, ASIO at 64 or 128 samples plays without dropouts.

Measure recording latency so takes line up

Take 5 moves every recording earlier by the delay the interface reports, so takes line up with what you played along to. Some interfaces report it wrongly, and a take then sits a few milliseconds late. To correct it exactly:

  1. Connect a cable from an output of the interface straight to an input. Turn monitors, amps and headphones down: the measurement plays clicks out of that output.
  2. Settings > Recording Latency...: choose that output (Out of) and input (Back in on). Under them it says what's applied now: "Not measured: going by what the interface reports", or the correction measured before.
  3. Press Measure: a short burst of clicks plays and Take 5 listens for a second and a half. It shows the round trip and how much more it is than the interface reports, and applies that.

The correction is kept for that input (by its name) between launches. Measure each input you record from, and again after changing the interface or its driver. If nothing comes back, check the cable, the levels and that both channels are ticked in Audio Settings (Measure is greyed while no input or output is on).

Connect a MIDI keyboard

  1. Plug in the keyboard (USB, or through the interface's MIDI in). Take 5 finds it by itself within a few seconds, even while it's open, and listens to every MIDI input it finds: there's nothing to tick. A Bluetooth keyboard pairs from Bluetooth MIDI... at the bottom of Settings > Audio Settings....
  2. Add an instrument track (Track > New Instrument Track..., CtrlAltSOptionCmdS) and choose an instrument from the menu. Instrument tracks listen to "All MIDI Ins", so any connected keyboard plays them.
  3. Arm the track (its R button) to play it live and record it.

No keyboard? Use MIDI > Musical Typing (CtrlKCmdK) to play from the computer keyboard: the letters play notes, Z and X change the octave, C and V the velocity, and Tab holds the sustain. If a keyboard plays some notes twice, see Settings > Ignore Notes Sent Twice By ("My keyboard plays some notes twice").

Send MIDI clock to a drum machine or another app

Settings > Send MIDI Clock To lists your MIDI outputs: tick one and it follows Take 5's tempo and start/stop (a drum machine plays along in time, an arpeggiator or another app keeps the tempo). Tick it again to stop sending. The choice is kept for that output between launches. The option is greyed out when no MIDI output is connected: plug in the device (or start the other app's virtual MIDI port: the IAC Driver in the Mac's Audio MIDI Setup; on Windows, a tool such as loopMIDI) and open the menu again. It shows up within a few seconds.

Use Bluetooth headphones (Mac)

Bluetooth headphones are fine for listening but lag too much for recording. Opening AirPods' (or another headset's) microphone switches them to a low-quality call mode, so when the output and input are the same Bluetooth headset, Take 5 records from the Mac's built-in microphone instead (or another input if there's none) and the headphones stay in music mode. It does this when Take 5 opens and when the headset connects while it's open. For recording, use wired headphones on an interface.

Customising

Change the look (themes)

View > Theme: Take 5 (the default: a studio look, near-black, with knobs and fader caps that look made, buttons that light up, slots and fields set into the panel, an LCD in soft green), Light, Graphite (the default before), Midnight, Slate, Stone, Charcoal, Steel, Indigo and Ember. Light and Stone are light; the others are dark. In every dark theme a knob's arc says what it does: amber for level (Gain, Threshold, Drive), blue for time and tone (Attack, Frequency), teal for blends (Mix, Width). In every theme a plugin slot's light is green when it's on, red when it's off (bypassed), and amber with a "!" if it crashed. Everything switches (tracks, mixer, editors, panels, menus, the window's title bar); the song stays open. The theme is remembered between launches and is the same in every layout (a layout doesn't change it). A theme only changes the look: the keys are in Settings > Key Commands... and the arrangement of the window in View > Layouts, so Steel with the Console layout and Set D's keys is three choices.

Make everything bigger or smaller

View > Display Size: 90%, 100% (the default), 110%, 125% or 150%. Everything scales and stays sharp; remembered after a relaunch. Useful on a big screen, from across the room, or for tired eyes. On a small laptop screen 90% fits more tracks and plugin slots. For taller or shorter tracks only, use View > Zoom > Taller Tracks / Shorter Tracks, Ctrl↑Cmd↑ / Ctrl↓Cmd↓: in steps of 20 points, between 60 and 280. The track height is kept with the layout.

Use another DAW's shortcuts

Settings > Key Commands...: under "Key set:", choose Take 5 (the default) or Set B to Set F, other sets of keys people know from elsewhere; the line under it shows the set's main keys (play, record, split, undo), so pick the one you recognise. Then change any single shortcut: click + to add a key, click a key to change or remove it. A key another command already has asks first (Re-assign moves it). A note under the list warns when a key is one the system takes first, so it would never reach Take 5 (on the Mac, Command-Space for Spotlight or Control+Arrows for Spaces; on Windows, Alt+F4, Ctrl+Esc and the like). The cheat sheet, tooltips, menus and tutorial follow your keys. Things to watch: choosing another key set replaces your single changes with that set's keys, and reset to defaults goes back to Take 5's keys (after asking; "Key set" then says Take 5). Your keys are kept in Take 5's settings, for every song.

Arrange the screen (layouts and panels)

  • View > Layouts: built-in arrangements (Default; Browser, the browser on the right and the mixer in place of the tracks; Edit and Mix, no panels and the MIDI editor in its own window; Compact, like Browser with shorter tracks; Mixer Below; Console; Browser and Mixer; Floating Windows, the mixer and the editor each in a window; Recording, big tracks; Mixing, the mixer full size), Save Current Layout... for your own, and Delete Saved Layout. A layout keeps: the side panels open and their order, whether they float or sit side by side, the mixer (shown or not, and where), the MIDI editor floating or not, which lanes show, the track height, the Inspector, the Console layout and the top bars' arrangement, the floating windows' places and the sizes of the panes. It doesn't change the theme, the display size or the keys. The Default layout opens the Shortcuts panel; the Console one also turns on View > Top Bars > Console Layout, the Inspector and the Audio File Browser.
  • Things to watch: choosing a built-in layout also puts its lanes and track height back; saving under a name you've used replaces that layout; Delete Saved Layout doesn't ask first.
  • Side panels (Shortcuts, Audio File Browser, Notes, Assistant, Test Checklist, Smart Controls, Modulators, CPU Use, in View > Panels) each open and close on their own key; several open at once stack on the right, one above another (drag the edge between two to share the height). View > Windows > Side Panels Side by Side puts them in columns; View > Windows > Side Panels in Own Windows floats them all (or, when they all float, puts them back).
  • Esc closes the panel or window you're working in, as its close button would: a side panel or the Inspector (once you've clicked in it, typing in its text box too), a panel in its own window, a plugin's window, the tuner and the other small windows, a dialog. A plugin's own controls may keep Esc to themselves: click the window's title bar first. While exporting, Esc in the export window cancels.
  • View > Windows > Mixer in Place of Tracks, Mixer Below Tracks and Mixer in Own Window say where the mixer goes; MIDI Editor in Own Window floats the MIDI editor.
  • Drag the edge between panes (tracks and mixer, the editor, the side panels, the track headers' right edge) to resize; double-click an edge for its default size. The track headers are 240 to 600 points wide (300 at first). Sizes are kept between launches and in saved layouts.

Arrange the top bars (Customize Top Bars)

The top of the window is made of blocks: the click and count-in, the transport's buttons, loop, punch and metronome, the position display (position, time, tempo, signature), the loudness, and the toolbar's undo and redo, M S L R W A, follow, the tools, snap and grid with Q, the output level, the zones, the status line and the info line. Put them where you like, in up to four rows:

  1. View > Top Bars > Customize Top Bars... (or right-click an empty part of the bars). Each block gets an outline, and a strip under the bars says how.
  2. Drag a block to move it: let go in the left, middle or right third of a row to put it at that row's left, in its middle or at its right (a line shows where it lands among the blocks there); let go under the last row to start a new row. A row you empty goes.
  3. Right-click a block to hide it; Show a Block... brings a hidden one back (at the end of the last row). Reset goes back to the layout's own arrangement.
  4. Done.

The arrangement is kept with the layout: save it with View > Layouts > Save Current Layout... to have it as a layout of your own. View > Top Bars > Toolbar, Status Line and Info Line hide those blocks, or bring them back on a row of their own, in any layout. In a window too narrow for a row, what doesn't fit goes onto a line under it.

Work in the Console layout

View > Top Bars > Console Layout (or View > Layouts > Console, which also opens the Inspector, the Audio File Browser and the mixer below the tracks, at a track height of 90) arranges the window around a toolbar and a console-style mixer, with any theme (View > Theme > Steel suits it). Choose it again to go back to Take 5's own.

  • The toolbar, under the transport, across the window. At the left: undo and redo; M (lit when a track is muted: click to unmute them all), S (the same for solos), L (the listen bus), R (every track reads its automation), W (every track writes it, in Touch; again, back to Read), A (the next track with automation); following the playhead. In the middle: the tools (pointer, marquee, scissors, glue, mute, zoom, draw, play); snapping on or off with its grid; Q to quantise. At the right: the output's level and the zones: the Inspector, the mixer below the tracks, the side panels. (Where the playhead is shows in the transport above.) In a narrow window the middle moves over, and what doesn't fit is left out.
  • The status line under it, across the window: whether the audio inputs and outputs are connected (amber when not), the listen bus, how long can be recorded on the song's disk, the record format, the pan law and the buffer size. Click one to change it (Audio Settings, the listen bus).
  • The info line under that, across the window in pills like the status line's: the selected clip's name, start, end, length, fades, volume and mute; with no clip selected, the selected track's name, volume, pan, mute and solo; a hint when nothing is. Several selected: how many, and the first one's. Click a value to type a new one, as in the Inspector; click Mute or Solo to switch it.
  • Hide any of these three rows with View > Top Bars > Toolbar, View > Top Bars > Status Line and View > Top Bars > Info Line; the tracks get the room. To move their blocks around (or onto the transport's row, or into Take 5's own layout), see "Arrange the top bars". They're kept with the layout.
  • Track headers: the track's number, M S, its name and level on top; under them record, monitor, R and W (this track's automation read, or written in Touch), e (its settings, in the Inspector) and A (its automation lanes); a picture of what it is; the meter down the right edge.
  • The mixer: under each name a Routing section (its input and output); Pre, the channel's input stage: Ø (polarity), its input gain (-24 to +24 dB) and a low cut (20 Hz to 2 kHz) and a high cut (1 to 20 kHz), 12 dB an octave, each switched on with LC or HC, before any plugin; then Inserts and Sends, each with a switch that turns all of them on or off at once and, for the inserts, the delay their plugins add (in ms); pan and width as bars; e beside M and S. The mixer's title bar has Configurations (save how the mixer looks under a name, and recall it), Racks (which of Routing, Pre, Inserts and Sends the strips show), Narrow / Wide strips (more fit when narrow), Q-Link (lit: moving a fader, pan, mute or send on one of the selected channels moves the others too, as Mix > Link Selected Channels), and M and S to unmute and unsolo everything.

Run several commands from one key (macros)

Settings > Macros...: New makes one ("Prepare a vocal track"), Rename... and Delete change the list. With a macro chosen, Add a Command... adds any command to it (from a list by menu: New Track, Arm Track, Input Monitoring, Channel Strip...), in order; Move Up, Move Down and Remove change its order, Run tries it. Set Key... opens Key Commands, where the macros are under Macros: give it a key there. Then the key (or Settings > Run Macro, greyed until there's a macro) runs its commands one after another. A command that can't run at that moment (Arm Track with no track selected, say) is skipped, and the status line says how many of them ran. A macro can't run another macro. Macros are kept in the app's settings, for every song.

Make stop go back to where I started playing

Settings > Stop Returns to Where Play Started: when on, stopping puts the playhead back where playing started; off (the default), it stays where it stopped. Transport > Go to Start, Return or Home, always goes to the start. Handy for playing the same passage again and again while mixing or rehearsing a part. After a scrub (playing while you drag the playhead), stop leaves it where it is. The setting is kept between launches.
